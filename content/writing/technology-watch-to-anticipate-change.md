In the various professions that media engineers practice today and will practice tomorrow, technology and practices evolve and will keep changing. This "evolution" is perceived as fast, uncontrolled and inevitable. With some hindsight and the right tools, it is possible to step back and see that it is not that fast, that it is possible to take control, and that there are moments when an evolution should be avoided.

### How the technology watch lab is designed

In the technology watch lab, we go through the different steps that make up a watch practice. Defining the field that truly interests us is essential: finding your *Ikigai*, the thing that gets you out of bed in the morning with a smile. Maintaining a list of the people who publish information and knowledge in your favorite field. Setting up the mechanisms to subscribe and receive what's new. Then configuring a sorting process and an automatic system to classify information. Deciding how and when to put this new knowledge into practice. And finally, when the time comes, making sure you can find the information again. Mastering this whole chain is essential to stay up to date and remain competitive in the professional world.

### What are the learning challenges?

Putting this chain of watch activities into practice is relatively easy to pick up. The two tricky aspects are rigor over time and the ability to question technological evolution. This questioning — the measure and the test of time — how do we pass it on to students? This skill and this critical mindset will be essential for the changes that lie ahead.

### Aviation as an example

History shows periods when technological evolution seemed inevitable but never actually materialized. Aviation, which saw passenger numbers and speed multiply every decade, is a perfect example. In the 1960s, during the space race, supersonic aircraft were destined to become the norm for all passenger transport in the years to come. So much so that the Boeing 747 (plan B) was designed to be converted into a cargo aircraft later on. And yet, the Concorde never took over. The oil crisis, noise, safety and pollution weighed too heavily against a few hours saved on an intercontinental flight that was already incredibly fast. Today, the return of supersonic aircraft is being announced despite the climate context.

### Standardization processes

Standardization initiatives are another fascinating topic. Take video conferencing, for example. Despite its explosive use during the pandemic, it is still not possible to call from Zoom to Teams. These proprietary systems without interoperability persist, while open standards such as WebRTC, Jabber, SIP, etc. have existed for years. Will we see one of the giants impose itself as the new standard and open up its protocol?

### A holistic approach is necessary

These two examples show that by focusing our magnifying glass on purely technological aspects, we miss the political, economic and societal stakes that influence advances and the march toward possible progress.

### How to connect it with theories of technological evolution

These stories allow a parallel with the different visualizations of technological evolution. Futurists like Roy Amara explain that we overestimate the effects of technology in the short term, while underestimating them in the long term. Or Gartner's much-criticized *hype* curve (a curve, because it's not really a cycle), which is nonetheless useful for understanding the disillusionment that follows the peak of inflated expectations. Just place blockchain or web3 on it to understand where we stand.

### Using a hot topic like artificial intelligence

Speaking of winter, it seems that the artificial intelligence one is coming to an end. The term didn't appear last week, and neither did its promises. Are we really witnessing a notable evolution? With the democratization of OpenAI's tools, DALL-E as much as ChatGPT, how will this change our behaviors? When I ask how to teach technology watch, here is what OpenAI answers:

> Technology watch consists of following news and developments in technology in order to stay informed and anticipate changes that could impact one's field of activity. Teaching technology watch can be useful to help students develop their ability to stay up to date on the latest technological advances and to identify the opportunities and challenges these changes may represent.
>
> Here are a few steps you can follow to teach technology watch:
>
> 1. Start by explaining what technology watch is and why it is important. Help students understand that technology watch can be a valuable asset to stay competitive and innovative in their field.
> 2. Make a list of important information sources to follow technology news. This can include specialized websites, professional journals, social networks, newsletters, etc.
> 3. Teach students how to use these information sources effectively. For example, how to subscribe to newsletters, use filters to sort information, etc.
> 4. Have students practice technology watch by asking them to find and share articles or news on a specific topic. This can be done in or outside the classroom.
> 5. Encourage students to get involved in online communities or attend technology-related events to strengthen their knowledge and their network.
> 6. Run reflection exercises to help students analyze and interpret the information they have collected. Ask them to think about the implications and opportunities that new technologies could have on their field of activity.
> 7. Encourage students to present their findings and reflections to the class or their peers, which will allow them to develop their presentation and communication skills.

What do you think? Rather impressive. I encourage students to use these new tools, even for their lab deliverable. Artificial intelligence, however, won't be able to answer their *Ikigai*. They must therefore use it with a critical eye — whether regarding the source of the model, the biases it implies, or the rights management of creations produced by the tool.

### Learning to learn

The role of school is above all to learn how to learn, and not only to teach the state of the art currently in force in the professional world. But an essential aspect of this learning is managing to grasp everything that surrounds technologies and daring to influence their evolution. With this course, I want our students not to settle for using Wikipedia, but to contribute to it.
